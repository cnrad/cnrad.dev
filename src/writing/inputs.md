---
title: Why should we care about the inputs?
date: Sept 28, 2026
---

I've been making music on the computer since I was a kid. Growing up, I had this idea that for a song to be my own, the majority of it had to be made from scratch, aside from a few drums or presets. I didn't particularly like the idea that someone could drag a bunch of pre-made loops together and call it a song they "made". It felt lazy and disingenuous to just slap together a bunch of someone else's work and call it my own, because that's not what "true" producers do.

I feel like something similar is happening with AI in software development. It's no longer about the process by which you create the output, but about having the output created and aligned with your vision. It got me thinking - if we have full ownership over the output, why should the inputs it took to get there matter?

A new medium of creation has emerged recently ("vibe-coding") that has abstracted away the need to look at the code most of the time. So, if we can make the software behave exactly how we want it to behave, there should be no problem - and if there is a problem, we can just prompt a fix, right? The days of writing clean code by hand in order to achieve good software are largely gone.

Perhaps one of the things you lose by abstracting this process is the sense of pride in your work.^1 It used to be that you'd spend days, weeks, or even months on figuring something out and fine-tuning it, making sure the code looked and worked great. After shipping something, you'd feel like you accomplished something, that it was *your* work out there in the world. 

With AI, I've found that sense of pride to be much less. I still take great care to make sure the output is exactly what I want it to be, but I didn't necessarily do any of the hard work it used to take to get there. It feels wrong to say "I" built something when I wrote little to none of the code, even though the intention was primarily driven by me. I think a lot of other people are struggling with this shift - it feels unfair that now virtually everybody can make things that only you knew how to make beforehand, without doing any of the hard work it used to require.

However, I've begun to look at it as an opportunity for a stronger sense of ownership. It's way faster now to generate the code, but you still need to have the taste^2 required to take something from good to polished. And because we're no longer writing the code by hand, that ownership and understanding of the intended behavior is even more valuable than it was before.

Interestingly, this shift in perspective has happened to me for music as well. I don't think using loops matters that much anymore - making a song sound good *does* still require skill (and taste).

The concept of work is changing - maybe we shouldn't care about the inputs at all, and instead focus on making the outputs as perfect as they can be.


^1 One could argue that you also lose a lot of context, since you aren't touching the code. I think this is mostly a skill issue though - there are ways to use these tools and still maintain a good level of ownership over the architecture / code.

^2 The term "taste" has been thrown around on X a lot recently. I consider taste to be the intuition required to make something feel polished, and the ability to articulate why you made those decisions.
